## H1: oil price rises worsen India's terms of trade. Supported.

**Say it like this:** "A 10% rise in the real oil price lowers India's terms of trade by about 2.4% in the same
year. The effect is fast but temporary: about a third of it unwinds every year."

| Estimate | Value | s.e. | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-run elasticity | −0.241 | 0.049 (HC1 0.040) | < 0.001 |
| Long-run elasticity | −0.092 | 0.059 | 0.12 |
| Long-run non-energy commodity elasticity | −0.249 | 0.177 | 0.16 |
| Error-correction α | −0.308 | 0.086 | 0.001 |

- **Statistical significance:** the short-run effect is significant at well below 1%, with or without robust
  standard errors, and in all six specifications (−0.19 to −0.28).
- **Economic significance:**
  - The 2022 oil rise (+83% in the fiscal-year average from FY2020 to FY2022) maps into roughly 0.24 × 83 ≈ 20%
    by the elasticity alone. The actual fall was 14% (goods and services) and 24% (merchandise).
  - So the elasticity gives the right order of magnitude for a real episode.
- **Why the long run is small:** the economy adjusts. Export prices of refined fuels follow crude with a lag, the
  rupee and export mix adjust, and oil spikes tend to reverse. That matches the time-horizon idea from L13: the
  short-run effect is bigger than the long-run effect.

**Monthly timing (t08).** "Almost nothing happens in the month oil rises. Two months later the terms of trade are
about 0.4% lower for every 1% rise in Brent, and they stay there."
- **Why the lag:** crude cargoes are priced on the previous month's benchmarks and recorded at customs when they
  arrive, often weeks after loading.

**The 2026 episode.**
- From February to April 2026, export unit values rose 18.1% and import unit values 15.7%. Refined-fuel export
  prices rose with crude.
- Up to June 2026 there is no clear fall in the terms of trade: April–June is not below November–January on
  either DGCI&S base (+5.5% on the 2012-13 base, +3.4% on the 2022-23 base, in three-month averages).
- So far, 2026 is the refining hedge at work. It is also a reminder not to read single months: from February to
  June, one DGCI&S base shows −13% and the other +8%.

## The size: theory benchmark and exposure over time

**Benchmark test (t07).**
- "Theory predicts a coefficient of 1. We get 1.70 and cannot reject 1 at 5%. Simple trade-share arithmetic from
  the definition of the terms of trade explains the direction and the size."
- The plain merchandise elasticity over the same years is −0.30, against an average benchmark of −0.16. The
  difference is the indirect channels: fertiliser, petrochemicals, plastics and freight.

**Rolling elasticity (t08b).**
- "India's exposure has roughly halved. A 10% oil rise used to cost about 3% of the terms of trade, and now it
  costs about 1.1%."
- **Why:** India's oil share of exports rose from about 3% (FY1990) to 9–22% (since FY2008). The benchmark fell
  from −0.25 to −0.10 over FY1999–2025, and the estimated elasticity followed it.
- **Supporting theory numbers:**
  - RCA in refined petroleum: 0.06 → 4.37.
  - Grubel–Lloyd for oil: 0.55 aggregated vs 0.10 separated.
  - Export/import unit value ratio: 1.13 to 1.41 (2000–2025).

## H2: rises and falls have unequal effects. Not supported.

**Say it like this:** "In the short run, a rise and a fall of the same size move the terms of trade by about the
same amount. The only asymmetry we find is in the long run, it depends on the 1970s, and it goes the opposite way
to what we expected."

| | Rise | Fall | p (symmetry) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short run, baseline | −0.27 | −0.22 | 0.62 |
| Short run, all 7 specifications | | | 0.54 – 0.91 |
| Long run, baseline | −0.16 | −0.23 | 0.035 |
| Long run, from FY1980 | −0.11 | −0.15 | 0.28 |

- **What it means:** India gains from cheap oil about as much as it loses from expensive oil. Policy should be
  symmetric too: save in the slumps to spend in the spikes.
- **Why the expectation failed:** Mork's asymmetry is about *output*. Output can respond asymmetrically because
  firms delay investment when prices are uncertain. The terms of trade is a *price ratio* that passes oil prices
  through almost mechanically in both directions.

## H3: supply-driven rises hurt more than demand-driven ones. Not supported.

**Say it like this:** "It matters *how much* oil rises more than *why*. Demand-driven rises hurt at least as much,
because a world boom raises the price of everything India imports, not just oil."

| Sample | Supply-driven | Demand-driven | p (equal) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual G&S, FY1975–2025 | −0.07 | −0.11 | 0.71 |
| Annual merchandise, FY1995–2024 | −0.03 | −0.13 | 0.55 |
| Monthly, 3-month cumulative | −0.24 | −0.37 | 0.53 |

- **Why the offer-curve intuition (case B) fails for India:** in a boom, India's other imports also get dearer
  (gold, coal, fertiliser, edible oils, metals). India's exports are mostly manufactures and services, whose
  prices rise less. So the rest of the world's offer curve moves against India on several goods at once.
- **The Känzig check:** pure OPEC news shocks lower the ToT by about 1% per shock annually and 4–5% after two to
  three months. Supply shocks clearly hurt, just not more than demand shocks.
- **Policy meaning:** it does not help India to ask whether a spike is "OPEC's fault" or a "global boom". The
  buffers are needed either way.

## Big-picture episodes (t10, fiscal-year averages)

| Episode | Real oil | ToT goods and services | ToT merchandise |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1973 OPEC embargo (FY1972 → FY1974) | +284% | −38.6% | – |
| 1979 Iranian revolution (FY1978 → FY1980) | +110% | −19.2% | – |
| 1990 Gulf war (FY1989 → FY1990) | +21% | −9.8% | – |
| 2008 price spike (FY2006 → FY2008) | +20% | +4.9% | +5.8% |
| 2014–16 collapse (FY2013 → FY2015) | −50% | −1.0% | +19.4% |
| 2020 COVID crash (FY2019 → FY2020) | −26% | +4.7% | +9.6% |
| 2022 Russia–Ukraine (FY2020 → FY2022) | +83% | −14.4% | −23.7% |

**2008 looks odd.** The spike lasted only months (Brent peaked in July 2008 and crashed by December), so the
fiscal-year average barely captured it. Monthly data would show it, but DGCI&S monthly data for 2008 are not
online. This is one reason we also use monthly data where we can.
