## A. The big picture

**A1. Why did you choose this topic?** *(P1)*\
India imports about 90% of the crude it refines, and oil is the largest single item in its import bill, about a
quarter of all imports on average. Almost every oil spike has come with stress on India's external accounts: 1990
before the 1991 crisis, 2013, 2022, and now March 2026. So the link between oil and what India's exports can buy is
one of the most practical questions in Indian international economics.

**A2. What is the terms of trade, in one line?** *(P1)*\
The price of what a country sells relative to the price of what it buys. We use the net barter terms of trade from
lecture 21: the export unit value index divided by the import unit value index, times 100.

**A3. Why study the terms of trade and not the trade balance?** *(P1)*\
The trade balance mixes prices, volumes, income and the exchange rate, so it is hard to say what an oil shock does
to it on its own. The terms of trade is the pure price channel, and it is what the trade theory in our course is
about: the offer-curve equilibrium is a terms-of-trade equilibrium. It is also the welfare channel: a worse terms of
trade is a real-income loss.

**A4. What is your main finding in one sentence?** *(P1)*\
A 10% oil price rise cuts India's terms of trade by about 2.4% within a year, mostly within two to three months,
whatever the cause of the rise, and India is about half as exposed as it was in the 1990s because it became a big
refined-fuel exporter.

**A5. What is new in your work?** *(P2)*\
Indian studies model the trade balance, inflation or the rupee; we model the terms of trade directly. We test it
against a benchmark derived from the definition of the terms of trade, we separate supply-driven from
demand-driven oil prices with identified shocks, we explain the fall in exposure with RCA and Grubel–Lloyd, and we
cover 2022 and 2026.

**A6. Why India? Wouldn't any oil importer look the same?** *(P1)*\
India is unusual in two ways. It is a large buyer (third-largest consumer, 5.4% of world demand), so it is not a
pure price-taker. And it is a big exporter of refined fuels (RCA of 4.37), so part of every oil shock comes back to
it through export prices. That hedge is what makes the size of India's exposure an empirical question.

**A7. Is a fall in the terms of trade always bad?** *(P1)*\
Not always. If export prices fall because India became more productive, it can still gain overall. But a fall
caused by an oil price spike is a pure loss: nothing improved in India, it simply has to give more exports for the
same oil.

**A8. What period do you cover, and why?** *(P2)*\
Annual data from FY1970-71 to FY2025-26, the longest consistent Indian series, which includes the 1973, 1979,
1990, 2008, 2014–16, 2020 and 2022 episodes. Monthly data from April 2019 to June 2026, which is all that DGCI&S
publishes online, and covers COVID, 2022 and the 2026 Hormuz shock.

**A9. What exactly happened in March 2026?** *(P1)*\
After the US and Israel struck Iran at the end of February, traffic through the Strait of Hormuz fell from about
20 million barrels a day to a trickle. The IEA's March Oil Market Report called it the largest oil supply
disruption in history. Brent rose 46% in a month, from $71 to $104, India's crude basket went from $69 to $113,
and the rupee ended March at 94.65 per dollar.

**A10. Who would use your results?** *(P4)*\
Anyone who has to plan for oil shocks: the Finance Ministry setting fuel taxes, the RBI judging how much a spike
will hurt the external accounts, and oil companies planning stocks. The rule of thumb is simple: roughly 0.25% of
the terms of trade lost per 1% oil rise in the short run, with the loss mostly in place within three months.

## B. Theory

**B1. Explain an offer curve.** *(P1)*\
It shows how much of its export good a country is willing to give in exchange for different amounts of its
import good. It comes from the trade triangles at each relative price, so it already contains the country's
production possibilities and preferences. Where India's and the rest of the world's curves cross is the trade
equilibrium, and the slope of the ray from the origin to that point is the terms of trade.

**B2. Why does India's offer curve bend towards the oil axis?** *(P1)*\
Because each extra unit of exports costs India more (increasing opportunity cost) and each extra unit of oil is
worth less to it (diminishing marginal utility). So India only offers more exports if it gets increasingly more oil
per unit, and the curve becomes steeper. Every offer curve bends towards the axis of the country's import good.

**B3. How is an OPEC+ supply cut like a tariff?** *(P1)*\
In lecture 22, a tariff makes the imposing country offer less trade at every price; its offer curve rotates
towards its import axis, the volume of trade falls and its terms of trade improve if it is large. A supply cut is
the oil exporter doing exactly that: it offers less oil for every unit of India's exports. So the oil exporter's
terms of trade improve and India's fall.

**B4. Where is the terms of trade in your diagram?** *(P1)*\
With India's exports on the horizontal axis and oil on the vertical axis, the ray's slope is oil per unit of
exports, which is P_X/P_Y. A flatter ray is worse for India. In our stylised diagram, the supply cut flattens it
from 1.00 to 0.86.

**B5. What happens in a world demand boom?** *(P1)*\
Two things at once. The rest of the world wants more oil, which pushes oil prices up, and it also wants more of
India's exports, which pushes India's export prices up. In the diagram both curves move, so theory cannot sign the
net effect. That is why H3 is an empirical question.

**B6. Is India a small country or a large country?** *(P1)*\
Large, in oil. It uses 5.4% of the world's oil, third after the US and China, so growth in India's demand can
raise the world price. That is case C on our slide. It means part of the oil price may be caused by India, which is
why we use shocks India cannot cause for H3.

**B7. What is the welfare cost of a worse terms of trade?** *(P1)*\
In lecture 11, a country consumes on its trade line, whose slope is the terms of trade. A worse terms of trade
rotates the line inwards, so the country reaches a lower indifference curve even if it produces the same. As a rough
size, net oil imports are about 3% of GDP, so a 10% oil rise costs India around 0.3% of GDP in purchasing power.

**B8. Derive your benchmark.** *(P2)*\
Write the export and import price indices as weighted averages of oil and non-oil prices, with oil weights s_x and
s_m. Take logs and differentiate, holding non-oil prices fixed: d ln P_X ≈ s_x d ln P_oil and d ln P_M ≈ s_m d ln
P_oil. Subtract: d ln NTT ≈ (s_x − s_m) d ln P_oil. With s_m ≈ 0.26 and s_x ≈ 0.10 on average, it is negative.

**B9. Your benchmark coefficient is 1.70, not 1. Is the theory wrong?** *(P2)*\
No. We cannot reject 1 at 5% (p = 0.07). It is a bit above 1 because the formula only counts oil itself, while oil
also raises the price of fertiliser, petrochemicals, plastics and freight, which India imports. Those indirect
channels make the real effect somewhat larger than the direct share.

**B10. What is RCA, and what did you find?** *(P2)*\
Balassa's index: a product's share in India's exports divided by its share in world exports. Above 1 means India
has a revealed comparative advantage. For refined petroleum it went from 0.06 in 1999 to 1.26 in 2000 and 4.37 in
2025, so refined fuels are over four times as important in India's exports as in world exports. For crude it is
about zero.

**B11. How can India have a comparative advantage in refining with no oil of its own?** *(P2)*\
It is an acquired advantage, not one from natural endowments. Refining has huge fixed costs, so scale cuts average
cost (lecture 16), and India has some of the largest and most complex refineries in the world on its west coast,
close to Gulf crude and to export markets. The scarce factor is capital and know-how, not crude.

**B12. What does the Grubel–Lloyd result show?** *(P2)*\
When crude and refined products are lumped together as "mineral fuels", GL is 0.55, which looks like a lot of
intra-industry trade. When we measure crude and products separately it falls to 0.10. That is exactly lecture 17's
warning: the broader the industry definition, the more trade looks intra-industry. India's oil trade is really
inter-industry: crude in, fuels out.

**B13. Is India's oil trade horizontal or vertical?** *(P2)*\
Vertical. Lecture 20's rule says trade is horizontal if export and import unit values are within ±15% of each
other. India's refined exports are worth 1.13 to 1.41 times its crude imports per tonne (2000–2025), outside that
band in every year except 2011 and 2012.
India imports a raw input and sells a processed, higher-value product.

**B14. What does the partial equilibrium model say about the oil import bill?** *(P2)*\
India's demand for crude is very inelastic in the short run. When the world supply curve shifts up, the price
rises a lot and the volume barely falls, so the import bill jumps. In our data the bill moves almost one-for-one
with the price (correlation 0.96), and the volume elasticity is only 0.16.

**B15. How does the monopoly model from lecture 16 apply?** *(P1)*\
A monopolist sets marginal revenue p(1 − 1/e) equal to marginal cost, so the markup is 1 over the elasticity of
demand. Short-run oil demand is very inelastic, so a dominant supplier like OPEC+ can raise prices a lot by
restricting output. That is the supply-shock case in our offer-curve diagram.

**B16. If oil demand is so inelastic, why doesn't OPEC+ raise prices forever?** *(P1)*\
Because the demand OPEC+ faces is the residual demand after other producers, and that is much more elastic. High
prices bring in US shale and other non-OPEC supply. Demand also becomes more elastic over time as people switch
fuels and cars, and cartel members cheat on quotas. A monopolist always ends up on the elastic part of its own
demand curve.

**B17. How does diversifying suppliers help India?** *(P4)*\
That is the pro-competitive effect in lecture 16: more sellers means less market power and a smaller markup. When
India buys from more countries, as it did by shifting a large share of its purchases to Russian crude after 2022,
no single seller can squeeze it as much, and it can buy at a discount.

**B18. How does Heckscher–Ohlin fit in?** *(P2)*\
In H-O, countries import goods that use their scarce factors intensively. India is poorly endowed with oil, a
natural resource, so crude imports are structural. That is why the exposure can be managed, through refining,
reserves and diversification, but not removed.

**B19. How do the time horizons in lecture 13 relate to your model?** *(P3)*\
Lecture 13 separates the very short run, where everything is fixed, from the medium run (specific factors) and the
long run (everything mobile). Our error-correction model has the same structure: the short-run elasticity of −0.24
is the effect before anything adjusts, α = −0.31 is how fast adjustment happens, and the long-run elasticity of
−0.09 is what is left afterwards.

**B20. Who gains and who loses inside India when oil rises?** *(P4)*\
In the specific-factors model, the factors tied to refining gain, because refined-product prices rise with crude.
Sectors that use oil heavily lose: transport, airlines, fertiliser, petrochemical users. Consumers lose purchasing
power, and the government faces a choice between higher prices, lower fuel taxes or bigger subsidies.

**B21. Isn't the real problem of an oil shock the trade deficit?** *(P1)*\
That is the mercantilist view from lecture 6. The deficit is how the loss is financed. The real loss is that each
unit of India's exports buys less oil, which is the terms-of-trade effect. A deficit can be financed by borrowing;
the lost purchasing power cannot be borrowed back.

**B22. What is immiserizing growth, and does it apply here?** *(P1)*\
Bhagwati's idea: if a country's growth worsens its terms of trade enough, the loss can exceed the gain from
growth. For India it would mean growth pushing up oil demand so much that the oil price rises against it. It needs
very inelastic foreign supply and a heavy dependence on trade, so it is a warning, not India's actual situation.

**B23. Why do freight costs matter for the terms of trade?** *(P2)*\
Imports are recorded CIF, including freight and insurance, while exports are recorded FOB, without them. When
war-risk insurance or freight rates jump, as in the Red Sea in 2024 or Hormuz in 2026, India's measured import
prices rise even if the oil price itself did not. That is lecture 14's transport-cost wedge, and we list it as a
limitation.

**B24. What is the Harberger–Laursen–Metzler effect?** *(P1)*\
A worse terms of trade lowers real income. If people smooth consumption, saving falls and the current account
worsens. It is the link between our result and India's balance-of-payments stress in 1990-91, 2013 and 2022.

**B25. Does Prebisch–Singer apply to India?** *(P1)*\
Prebisch–Singer says commodity exporters face a long-run decline in their terms of trade against manufactures.
India is on the other side in oil: it imports the commodity. So falling commodity prices help it, which is what we
see in 2014–16, when the merchandise terms of trade rose 19%.

**B26. Does the Marshall–Lerner condition matter here?** *(P1)*\
It says a depreciation improves the trade balance only if export and import demand are elastic enough. Oil demand
is very inelastic, so a weaker rupee does little to cut the oil bill in the short run; it mainly raises the rupee
cost. That is part of why oil shocks and rupee weakness come together, and why we model the price channel directly.

## C. Literature

**C1. Why so few references?** *(P2)*\
We kept it to the papers we use: four strands, about 16 core references. For a 15-minute talk, it is better to
know each paper well than to list fifty. Every reference was checked against its DOI or official page.

**C2. What is your anchor paper, and how do you differ from it?** *(P2)*\
Backus and Crucini (2000), "Oil prices and the terms of trade". They use a general-equilibrium model for
industrial countries and find oil explains much of the variation in their terms of trade. We take the same
question to India, which is an emerging importer and a refined-fuel exporter, and answer it empirically, with a
theory benchmark and identified shocks.

**C3. What did Kilian (2009) show?** *(P2)*\
That "not all oil price shocks are alike". He separates oil supply shocks, global demand shocks and oil-specific
demand shocks, and shows they have different effects. Much of the 2000s price rise was demand-driven. It is the
reason we ask H3.

**C4. What do Baumeister and Hamilton (2019) do differently?** *(P3)*\
Kilian assumes oil supply cannot respond to price within a month. Baumeister and Hamilton replace that strict
assumption with Bayesian priors on the supply and demand elasticities. They find supply disruptions matter more
than Kilian's method implies. We use their shock series to split oil price changes.

**C5. What is Känzig's oil supply news shock?** *(P4)*\
Känzig (2021, American Economic Review) measures how oil futures prices move in a narrow window around OPEC
announcements. Because the window is so short, the move reflects only news about future supply. A negative news
shock raises oil prices immediately. India cannot cause these shocks, so they are a clean test.

**C6. What did Hamilton and Mork find about asymmetry?** *(P4)*\
Hamilton (1983) showed oil price rises came before most US post-war recessions. Mork (1989) found that rises hurt
output but falls did not help as much. That is the basis of H2. For India's terms of trade we find no such
asymmetry.

**C7. What does Indian research say?** *(P2)*\
Bhanumurthy, Das and Bose (2012) trace oil shocks through import, price and fiscal channels. Ghosh (2011) finds
higher oil prices depreciate the rupee. Tiwari and Olayeni (2013) show the oil–trade balance link changes across
time horizons. Deheri and Sahu (2024) find oil-specific demand shocks worsen India's trade balances. None models
the terms of trade directly.

**C8. Deheri and Sahu say demand-type shocks matter for India. Does that agree with you?** *(P4)*\
Broadly yes. They look at the trade balance and find oil-specific demand shocks are the ones that matter. We look at
the terms of trade and find demand-driven rises hurt at least as much as supply-driven ones. Both say the source
of the shock does not make supply shocks the special danger for India.

**C9. Schmitt-Grohé and Uribe find terms-of-trade shocks explain under 10% of output swings. So why care?** *(P2)*\
They ask how much output moves. We ask how much real purchasing power moves, which is the terms of trade itself.
For India, terms-of-trade swings of 14–24% in a single oil episode are large real-income shocks, whatever they do
to output in the short run.

## D. Data

**D1. Where does your data come from?** *(P2)*\
Only official agencies and the published data of peer-reviewed papers: the RBI Handbook of Statistics, DGCI&S,
PPAC, the World Bank, UNCTAD, UN Comtrade, the US EIA and the BIS, plus the Baumeister–Hamilton and Känzig shock
series. Every file is listed with its web address and a checksum in `data/SOURCES.md`.

**D2. What is a unit value index, and how is it different from a price index?** *(P2)*\
A unit value is the value of a commodity divided by its quantity, for example rupees per tonne. DGCI&S combines
them into Laspeyres indices with fixed base-year weights. A true price index would track the same product over
time; a unit value also changes when the quality or mix within a commodity code changes. It is the best long
official measure for India, but it is not a pure price.

**D3. Why two measures of the terms of trade?** *(P2)*\
The national-accounts measure (export deflator over import deflator, goods and services) goes back to 1970 on one
method, so it covers all the big oil shocks. The merchandise measure (DGCI&S unit values) is the textbook goods
definition but only links cleanly from FY1994. If both give the same answer, the result does not depend on one
statistical source.

**D4. Why do the two measures sometimes disagree, as in 2014–16?** *(P2)*\
The merchandise measure is goods only, where oil is about a quarter of imports. The goods-and-services measure
adds services, which have grown from a fifth of India's exports in 1990 to almost half today (WDI). Their prices
do not move with oil, so the oil effect is diluted.
The two are also built differently: unit values versus national-accounts deflators.

**D5. How did you handle the change in base years?** *(P2)*\
RBI publishes the merchandise series on 1978-79, 1999-2000 and 2012-13 bases. For each year we use the newest base
available and link the older ones at the overlap year with a ratio. That keeps each base's growth rates and only
rescales the level. It is the standard overlap-ratio method.

**D6. Why not just use the old 1978-79 base series? It is longer.** *(P2)*\
Because it contradicts the newer base. Over FY1999 to FY2007, the 1978-79 base shows the terms of trade rising
21.7% while the 1999-2000 base shows it falling 21.0%. The old weights are from before India imported much oil or
exported any refined fuel, so they misweight exactly the goods we study.

**D7. You recompute the terms of trade instead of using RBI's published numbers. Why?** *(P2)*\
Because three of RBI's published values (FY2002-03, 2015-16 and 2018-19) do not equal RBI's own export index divided
by its import index, beyond rounding. The definition in lecture 21 is the ratio of the two indices, so we compute
it from them. Everywhere else, the recomputed and published values agree.

**D8. How did you find the error in RBI Table 32?** *(P2)*\
The crude volumes for the 1990s were far too high: they matched the 2000s, when India imported several times more
crude. On inspection, the eight rows for FY1990-91 to 1997-98 were an exact copy of FY2000-01 to 2007-08 in all
four columns. We set them to missing, and the validation script now checks every table for repeated blocks of rows.

**D9. How did you align fiscal years and calendar years?** *(P2)*\
Indian data are April to March. We average monthly world prices over April to March in code. For the MUV deflator,
which is only calendar-year, we use 0.75 of year t plus 0.25 of year t+1, because nine months of the fiscal year
fall in t. We also checked that WDI's "2024" for India is fiscal year 2024-25.

**D10. Why do you use the real oil price, and why deflate by MUV?** *(P3)*\
The real price is what matters for the terms of trade: if all prices doubled, the terms of trade would not change.
MUV is the World Bank's index of manufactured export prices, so oil divided by MUV is the price of oil in terms of
manufactures. That is the relative price on the axes of the offer-curve diagram, and it is the World Bank's
standard deflator for commodity prices.

**D11. Why not use the Indian crude basket?** *(P2)*\
PPAC's usable monthly series only starts in 2020, and its website does not even have the FY2022-23 file. Our main
price, the average of Brent, Dubai and WTI from the World Bank, goes back to 1960. The basket tracks Dubai and Brent
closely, and our results are the same with Brent alone.

**D12. What is the Indian crude basket?** *(P2)*\
PPAC's weighted average of the sour Gulf grades (Dubai and Oman) and sweet Brent-type crude, in the proportion
India's refiners actually buy them. It is mostly sour crude, which is why it spiked more than Brent in March 2026:
$113 against $104.

**D13. Why do your monthly data only start in 2019?** *(P2)*\
DGCI&S's online archive for the 2012-13 base starts in FY2019-20, and the older 1999-2000 base page returns "No
Data Found". We did not want to type numbers in from printed reports. So the monthly sample is 87 months, and the
long annual series carry the history.

**D14. Isn't 54 years too few for a time-series model?** *(P3)*\
It is small, which is why we chose ARDL (it behaves well in small samples), kept lags to at most 2, and used
finite-sample critical values. The short-run result is significant at well below 1% in every specification, so
sample size is not what drives it.

**D15. Did anyone check the data by hand?** *(P2)*\
Three layers. The validation script cross-checks the sources against each other, with zero failures. A separate
script re-reads every raw file with its own code, re-estimates every model and compares each number we quote: all
of them match (`docs/number_check.md`). And we downloaded the main sources again from the RBI, World Bank, FRED and
Känzig's site and compared them with our copies: identical, every number. `data/validation_log.md` also lists
eleven values to tick off by hand against the original publications.

**D16. How do you know World Bank and FRED oil prices are the same?** *(P2)*\
We compared them: correlation 0.9998 and a mean difference of 33 cents a barrel over 441 months. The PPAC basket
lies between Dubai and Brent in 95% of normal months.

**D17. Are your 2026 data final?** *(P2)*\
No. The latest months are provisional and may be revised. That is why the 2026 episode is shown as an illustration
and the main estimates use the long annual series. We say on the limitations slide that the 2026 shock is still
unfolding.

**D18. What does a value like "FY2024" mean in your tables?** *(Any)*\
The fiscal year that starts in April 2024, so 2024-25. All our annual Indian data use that labelling.

**D19. DGCI&S now publishes a 2022-23 base. Why do your monthly models use the 2012-13 base?** *(P2)*\
Because the new base only starts in April 2023, which is 39 months, too short for our monthly models. We did compare
the two: month to month they hardly agree (the correlation of their monthly changes is about zero), and from
February to June 2026 one shows −13% and the other +8%. That told us single months are mostly noise from the mix of
goods, so we never judge an episode on one month. Our main results rest on annual data and on regressions over many
months.

## E. Econometrics

**E1. Why is everything in logs?** *(P3)*\
So the coefficients are elasticities: the percentage change in the terms of trade for a 1% change in the oil price.
That makes them comparable across periods and with the theory benchmark, which is also an elasticity. Logs also
make the very large 1970s price jumps less dominant.

**E2. What is a spurious regression?** *(P3)*\
If two series both wander or trend, a regression of one on the other can look highly significant even when they
are unrelated, because both are just drifting. That is why we test the order of integration first and use a model
built for non-stationary data.

**E3. What does the ADF test do?** *(P3)*\
It tests whether a series has a unit root, meaning shocks to it are permanent. The null is a unit root; rejecting
it means the series is stationary. "Augmented" means lagged differences are added to soak up serial correlation.

**E4. Why also KPSS?** *(P3)*\
Its null is the opposite: the series is stationary. ADF has low power in short samples, so failing to reject a unit
root is weak evidence on its own. Running both tells us where each series really stands. For us: levels are
borderline, first differences are clearly stationary.

**E5. What does Zivot–Andrews add?** *(P3)*\
A series with a one-time break, such as after 1991, can look like it has a unit root when it does not. Zivot–Andrews
allows one break chosen by the data. It still does not reject a unit root for our levels (breaks found in 1985 for
the terms of trade and 1999 for oil), so a single break is not what drives our results.

**E6. Why ARDL rather than OLS in levels?** *(P3)*\
OLS in levels risks a spurious regression with non-stationary data. OLS in differences throws away the long-run
information. ARDL in error-correction form keeps both: the short-run effect from the differences and the long-run
relationship from the lagged levels.

**E7. Why ARDL and not Johansen?** *(P3)*\
Johansen needs all variables to be I(1), and ours are borderline between I(0) and I(1). The ARDL bounds test is
valid either way. It also works better in small samples like our 54 years, and we have a clear dependent variable,
the terms of trade, so a single equation is natural.

**E8. How did you choose the lags?** *(P3)*\
By the Akaike information criterion, searching up to two lags of each variable. It picked ARDL(1,1,1): one lag of
the terms of trade and one of each regressor. More than two lags with 54 observations would use up too many degrees
of freedom.

**E9. What does α = −0.31 mean?** *(P3)*\
It is the speed of adjustment. If the terms of trade is away from its long-run relationship with oil, 31% of the
gap closes in the next year. That is a half-life of about two years. It has the right sign (negative) and is between
−1 and 0, which a valid error-correction model needs.

**E10. Explain the bounds test and your result.** *(P3)*\
It is an F-test that all the lagged levels are jointly zero, which means there is no long-run relationship. The
critical values come as a lower bound (all regressors I(0)) and an upper bound (all I(1)). Our F of 4.74 is above
the 10% upper bound of 4.32 but between the 5% bounds of 4.02 and 5.13. So there is a long-run relationship at 10%,
and the 5% test is inconclusive.

**E11. Why finite-sample critical values?** *(P3)*\
The original Pesaran–Shin–Smith critical values are for large samples. With 54 observations they are too lenient:
you would find cointegration too often. Kripfganz and Schneider (2020) give critical values and p-values for the
actual sample size, so ours are the stricter, more honest version.

**E12. Which case of the bounds test did you use?** *(P3)*\
Case 3: an unrestricted intercept and no trend. The terms of trade has no significant trend over 1970–2025 (110 in
FY1970, 98 in FY2025), so an intercept is enough.

**E13. The long-run elasticity is not significant. Doesn't that undermine H1?** *(P3)*\
No. H1 is about whether oil rises worsen the terms of trade, and the short-run elasticity of −0.24 is significant at
well below 1% in every specification. What the long run tells us is that the effect is temporary: the economy
largely adjusts. We say that openly instead of overselling a long-run result.

**E14. How do you get a standard error for the long-run elasticity?** *(P3)*\
It is a ratio of two estimates, −β₁/α, so we use the delta method: a first-order Taylor approximation that combines
the variances and the covariance of β₁ and α. It gives 0.059.

**E15. You found heteroskedasticity. What does it change?** *(P3)*\
Heteroskedasticity does not bias the coefficients, only the usual standard errors. So we also report HC1 robust
standard errors. The short-run standard error goes from 0.049 to 0.040, so the result gets, if anything, stronger.

**E16. What do CUSUM and CUSUMSQ show?** *(P3)*\
They track the cumulative sum of recursive residuals (and of their squares). If the coefficients changed during the
sample, the line would cross the 5% bands. Ours stay inside, so the relationship is stable over 1970–2025.

**E17. What does the RESET test check?** *(P3)*\
Whether powers of the fitted values add explanatory power, which would mean the functional form is wrong (for
example, a missing non-linearity). p = 0.84, so there is no sign of that.

**E18. What is the R² of your ARDL, and is it low?** *(P3)*\
0.51 (adjusted 0.45) for an equation in first differences, explaining year-to-year changes in the terms of trade.
For annual changes that is respectable, and the question is not whether we explain everything but whether the oil
effect is significant and stable.

**E19. Isn't the oil price endogenous?** *(P3)*\
Partly, because India is a big buyer. In annual data India's own demand moves world prices only slowly, and the
oil price is mostly driven by world supply and demand. We handle it directly in H3 with Baumeister–Hamilton and
Känzig shocks, which India cannot cause, and those give the same picture.

**E20. Which controls did you use, and why not more?** *(P3)*\
The real non-energy commodity price, which separates oil from a general commodity boom. A robustness check adds the
gold price. With 54 years we cannot add many controls, and some, like the exchange rate, respond to oil themselves.
Controlling for them would remove part of the effect we want to measure.

**E21. Oil and other commodity prices move together. Is that multicollinearity?** *(P3)*\
They are correlated, about 0.47 in annual changes, but far from perfectly. The test is whether the oil coefficient
is stable when we drop the control: it is about −0.20 without it and −0.24 with it, both highly significant.

**E22. Explain NARDL.** *(P4)*\
It splits the oil price into two running totals, one of all the increases and one of all the decreases, and puts
both in the ARDL. Then a rise and a fall can have different coefficients, and a Wald test checks whether they are
equal, separately for the short run and the long run.

**E23. How do you read the signs in NARDL?** *(P4)*\
The "falls" total only ever goes down. A negative coefficient on it means a fall in oil prices raises the terms of
trade. So both coefficients negative means "rises hurt, falls help", and asymmetry is about whether they have the
same size.

**E24. Why do you call the long-run asymmetry "not robust"?** *(P4)*\
In the baseline it is significant at 5% (p = 0.035), but it disappears with small changes: p = 0.05 with one more
lag, 0.08 from 1975, 0.28 from 1980, and it reverses in the merchandise data. A result that depends on including the
1970s is not something we would base policy on.

**E25. What is a local projection?** *(P3)*\
Instead of estimating one dynamic model and simulating it forward, like a VAR, you run a separate regression for
each horizon: the change in the terms of trade from month t−1 to month t+h on the oil price change in month t. The
coefficient at each horizon is the cumulative response. It is Jordà's 2005 method.

**E26. Why local projections and not a VAR?** *(P3)*\
With about 85 months, a VAR's impulse responses depend on getting the whole lag structure right, and an error at
one lag affects every horizon. Local projections estimate each horizon directly and are more robust to that. The
cost is wider confidence bands.

**E27. Why HAC standard errors with h + 1 lags?** *(P3)*\
At horizon h, the left-hand side overlaps across consecutive months, so the errors are autocorrelated by
construction, up to h lags. Newey–West (HAC) standard errors correct for that.

**E28. Explain the H3 method.** *(P4)*\
Two steps. First, regress each month's oil price change since 1975 on Baumeister and Hamilton's four structural
shocks and their lags. The part explained by supply shocks is the supply-driven change; the part explained by the
activity, consumption and inventory shocks is the demand-driven change. Second, regress India's terms-of-trade
changes on the two parts and test whether their coefficients are equal.

**E29. Isn't there a generated-regressor problem?** *(P4)*\
Yes, in principle: the two parts are estimated, so second-step standard errors are a bit too small. But the first
step is very precise (612 months, R² of 0.80), and our conclusion is that the two effects are *not* different.
Correcting the standard errors would make them larger, which only strengthens that conclusion.

**E30. What does R² = 0.80 in the first step mean?** *(P4)*\
The four structural shocks explain 80% of the month-to-month variation in the oil price since 1975. So the split
into supply and demand parts covers most of what moves oil.

**E31. Why are Känzig's shocks exogenous to India?** *(P4)*\
They are measured from oil futures price moves in a very short window around OPEC announcements. Nothing India
does in that window causes the move. So when India's terms of trade falls after one of these shocks, the direction
of cause is clear.

**E32. How do you deal with structural breaks, like the 1991 reforms?** *(P3)*\
Three ways. Zivot–Andrews allows a break in the unit-root test. CUSUM and CUSUMSQ show the coefficients are stable.
And the post-1980 subsample and the rolling regressions show how the effect changed. The rolling estimate changes
gradually, as refining capacity grew, not in a sudden jump.

**E33. The rolling windows overlap. Can you test the decline?** *(P4)*\
Overlapping windows are not independent, so we treat the rolling chart as descriptive. The test is that the decline
matches the theory benchmark built from trade shares, which is completely separate data, and that the first and last
windows (1970–89 and 2005–24) do not overlap at all: −0.30 (s.e. 0.09) against −0.11 (s.e. 0.06).

**E34. Why do the monthly and annual elasticities differ (−0.39 vs −0.24)?** *(P3)*\
They measure different things. The monthly one is merchandise only, where oil is a bigger share, and the plain
merchandise elasticity in annual data is −0.30, close to it. The annual goods-and-services measure is diluted by
services. Annual averaging also smooths short spikes.

## F. Results

**F1. Interpret −0.24 in plain words.** *(P3)*\
If the real oil price rises 10% this year, India's terms of trade fall about 2.4% this year: each unit of India's
exports buys 2.4% less imports than before.

**F2. Why is the short-run effect bigger than the long-run effect?** *(P3)*\
In the short run, contracts and demand are fixed, so the higher oil price passes straight into import prices. Over
time, refined-fuel export prices catch up with crude, the export mix and the rupee adjust, and oil spikes tend to
reverse. That is lecture 13's time-horizon logic.

**F3. Why does the effect take two to three months?** *(P3)*\
Crude cargoes are priced on the previous month's benchmark and recorded at customs when they arrive, often weeks
after loading. So a price rise in March shows up in India's import unit values in April and May.

**F4. Your model says oil hurts within two to three months. Why haven't India's terms of trade clearly fallen in 2026?** *(P4)*\
Because India's refined-fuel exports rose in price too. From February to April 2026, export unit values rose 18.1%,
as fast as import unit values (15.7%). In three-month averages, April–June is not below November–January on either
DGCI&S base. That is the refining hedge, and it is why exposure has halved. The data stop in June and single months
are noisy, so it is too early to call the full effect.

**F5. What did 2022 look like?** *(P4)*\
From February to June 2022, Brent rose 25% and the merchandise terms of trade fell to its lowest point in our
monthly data (89.4 in June 2022). In fiscal-year averages, FY2022 was 14.4% below FY2020 for goods and services and
23.7% below for merchandise.

**F6. Why does 2008 show an improvement?** *(P4)*\
The 2008 spike lasted only months: oil peaked in July and collapsed by December. A fiscal-year average barely
captures it, and the following crash falls in the same fiscal year. Monthly data would show it, but DGCI&S monthly
data for 2008 are not online.

**F7. How sure are you that exposure has halved?** *(P4)*\
Two independent pieces of evidence agree. The rolling regression goes from −0.30 to −0.11, and the trade-share
benchmark, built from completely different data, goes from −0.25 to −0.10. And the mechanism is visible in the RCA
for refined fuels, which went from 0.06 to 4.37.

**F8. H2 was not supported. Does that mean oil shocks are never asymmetric?** *(P4)*\
No. Mork's asymmetry is about output, where firms can delay investment when oil prices are volatile. The terms of
trade is a price ratio that passes oil prices through almost mechanically in both directions. So it is quite
consistent for output to be asymmetric and the terms of trade to be symmetric.

**F9. H3 was not supported. Were you surprised?** *(P4)*\
At first, yes, because the offer-curve story suggested a boom would help India's exports. But a world boom raises
the prices of all commodities, and India imports many of them: gold, coal, fertiliser, edible oils, metals. India's
exports are mostly manufactures and services, whose prices rise less. So demand-driven rises hurt at least as much.

**F10. Two of your three hypotheses were rejected. Did the project fail?** *(P1)*\
No. A hypothesis test is supposed to be able to fail. We learned that what matters for India is how much oil rises,
not why, and that rises and falls matter equally, which changes the policy advice: India needs symmetric buffers
that work whatever the cause of the shock.

**F11. What surprised you most?** *(Any)*\
That India's exposure has halved, and that the simple trade-share formula from the definition of the terms of trade
tracks it so closely. We expected refining to matter, but not that a one-line formula would get the size right.

**F12. How reliable is the monthly −0.39 with only 85 months?** *(P3)*\
The bands are wide, but the effect is significant at every horizon from one to six months. For example, at two
months it is −0.39 with a standard error of 0.13, and at three months −0.37 with 0.08. Month 0 is close to zero,
which is also what we expected.

**F13. What does the benchmark coefficient of 1.70 tell us?** *(P2)*\
That the trade-share arithmetic gets the direction and order of magnitude right, and we cannot reject that it is
exactly right (p = 0.07). The point estimate above 1 says the real effect is a bit bigger than the direct oil
channel, because of the indirect cost of oil in fertiliser, petrochemicals and freight.

**F14. If oil prices fell 30% tomorrow, what would happen to India's terms of trade?** *(P3)*\
Since effects are symmetric, roughly 0.24 × 30 ≈ 7% better in the goods-and-services measure within a year, and more
in the merchandise measure, mostly within two to three months. Then part of it would fade as the economy adjusts.

**F15. What would your model say about FY2026?** *(P4)*\
It depends on how long prices stay high and whether refined-fuel export prices keep pace. So far (to June) they
have, and the monthly data show no clear fall. If the fiscal-year average oil price ends well above FY2025's, the
annual model points to a fall of roughly a quarter of that percentage rise, with recovery as prices normalise.

## G. Policy and current affairs

**G1. What should India do?** *(P4)*\
Four things. Build buffers that work both ways, such as strategic reserves and a fuel-tax buffer, because rises and
falls matter equally. Keep growing the refining hedge. Diversify crude suppliers to weaken any one seller's market
power. And cut overall commodity dependence through the energy transition, because demand-driven booms hurt too.

**G2. What is a fuel-tax buffer?** *(P4)*\
Cut fuel duties when oil spikes and rebuild them when oil is cheap, so consumers face smoother prices and the
government's revenue absorbs the swing. India did a version of this: excise duties were raised in 2020 when oil
crashed and cut in November 2021 and May 2022 when it spiked.

**G3. Does India have strategic oil reserves?** *(P4)*\
Yes: underground caverns at Visakhapatnam, Mangaluru and Padur, about 5.3 million tonnes, which is roughly 9–10
days of consumption. Together with the oil companies' own stocks, the cover is longer. Our results say reserves are
worth more than they look, because they help during the two-to-three-month window when the terms-of-trade hit lands.

**G4. Did buying Russian crude after 2022 help?** *(P4)*\
It is the pro-competitive effect from lecture 16: more suppliers and a discount. Because DGCI&S unit values record
the prices India actually paid, any discount is already in our import price index. We did not estimate its size
separately, so we would not put a number on it.

**G5. Should India restrict refined-fuel exports during a spike to protect domestic supply?** *(P4)*\
That would weaken the hedge. Refined exports are what keep India's export prices rising with oil, which is why
exposure has halved. Restricting them would raise s_m − s_x again. There is a domestic-supply trade-off, but in terms
of the terms of trade, export restrictions make things worse.

**G6. What about windfall taxes on fuel exports, like in 2022?** *(P4)*\
India introduced special duties on fuel exports and domestic crude in July 2022. They raise revenue, but taxing fuel
exports reduces what India earns from the refining hedge during the very period it matters most. Our results would
argue for keeping such taxes temporary.

**G7. How does ethanol blending fit in?** *(P4)*\
Blending ethanol into petrol replaces some imported crude with a domestic product, which lowers s_m. And since we find
demand-driven commodity booms hurt too, reducing commodity-import dependence in general, through renewables and
electric transport, is the long-run answer.

**G8. Would paying for oil in rupees help?** *(P4)*\
It reduces exposure to the dollar, but not to the oil price. The terms of trade is a ratio of prices; whether
India pays in rupees or dollars, a 46% jump in the oil price still means each unit of exports buys less oil.

**G9. How did the Red Sea attacks in 2024 matter?** *(P2)*\
Ships rerouted around Africa, and freight and insurance costs rose. Since India's imports are valued including
freight (CIF) and exports without it (FOB), that raises measured import prices and worsens the measured terms of
trade, even without an oil price change. That is lecture 14's transport-cost wedge.

**G10. What does your work say about the rupee in 2026?** *(P4)*\
We do not model the exchange rate directly. The rupee's weakest year-end rate on record, 94.65 per dollar at end-March 2026, is consistent with
the literature (Ghosh 2011): oil shocks raise India's import bill, which weakens the rupee. A weaker rupee does not
change the terms of trade much, because both export and import prices are mostly set in dollars.

**G11. If you advised the RBI, what would you say?** *(P4)*\
Expect most of the terms-of-trade hit within two to three months of an oil spike, about 0.25% per 1% rise in the
short run, and expect the same size of gain when oil falls. Use the gains in good times to build reserves.

**G12. Does the energy transition make this question irrelevant?** *(P4)*\
Not soon. Oil is still more than a fifth of India's imports (22% in FY2025-26), and India's oil demand is growing. Over decades, lower oil
use would shrink s_m and the exposure, which is the same direction refining has already moved it.

## H. Limitations and extensions

**H1. What is your biggest limitation?** *(Any)*\
Unit value indices are not true price indices: a change in the mix or quality of goods within a commodity code moves
them too. It affects every study of India's terms of trade, and both of our measures (unit values and national-
accounts deflators) give the same answer.

**H2. What else would you list?** *(Any)*\
The monthly sample is short (87 months). The goods-and-services measure dilutes oil with services. CIF/FOB valuation
lets freight shocks move the measured terms of trade. And 2026 data are provisional.

**H3. Could gold be driving your results?** *(P3)*\
Gold is a big Indian import and its price moves with commodity booms. So one robustness check adds the real gold
price. The short-run oil elasticity is −0.22 with gold included, still highly significant.

**H4. What would you do with more time?** *(Any)*\
Digitise older DGCI&S monthly reports to extend the monthly series back before 2019. Use commodity-level unit values
to split the effect into crude, products and other commodities. Use Känzig's shock as an instrument for the oil price
in the local projections (LP-IV). And link the terms-of-trade effect to the current account, the HLM channel.

**H5. Do your results apply to other countries?** *(Any)*\
The method does, the numbers do not. The benchmark (s_x − s_m) tells you what to expect for any country: an oil
importer with no refining exports, like Bangladesh, should be more exposed; one that also exports products, like
South Korea, should look more like India today.

**H6. Isn't the refining "hedge" just mechanical, since fuel prices follow crude?** *(P2)*\
Yes, and that is exactly why it works. Because refined-fuel export prices move with crude, a bigger fuel export share
means more of each oil shock comes back to India as higher export prices. The value added on top (the 1.2 to 1.4
unit-value ratio) is what India earns from refining.

**H7. You did not include the exchange rate. Isn't that a missing variable?** *(P3)*\
The terms of trade is a ratio of two prices that are mostly set in dollars, so the exchange rate largely cancels out.
And the exchange rate reacts to oil shocks itself, so controlling for it would absorb part of the oil effect we want
to measure.

**H8. Why not use a VAR for the annual data?** *(P3)*\
With 54 years, a VAR with three or four variables and two lags would have too many parameters for the data. ARDL is
much more economical and answers our question directly.

## I. Curveballs

**I1. Can you draw the offer-curve diagram for us?** *(P1)*\
Axes: India's exports X across, oil Y up. Draw India's curve bending up towards the Y axis and the rest of the
world's curve bending towards the X axis; mark where they cross and draw the ray from the origin. For a supply cut,
rotate the rest-of-world curve towards the X axis; the new crossing has a flatter ray, which is India's worse terms
of trade.

**I2. What is the difference between the terms of trade and the real exchange rate?** *(P1)*\
The terms of trade compares India's export prices with its import prices. The real exchange rate compares India's
overall price level, or its tradable goods, with other countries' prices, adjusted for the nominal exchange rate.
An oil shock worsens the terms of trade directly; the real exchange rate moves through the rupee and inflation.

**I3. Your slides and tables show different standard errors for the same number. Why?** *(P3)*\
The table shows both the usual and the heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors. For the short-run elasticity
they are 0.049 and 0.040. The conclusions are the same with either.

**I4. What software did you use?** *(P3)*\
Python: pandas for data, statsmodels for ARDL, OLS, HAC and local projections, the arch package for unit-root
tests, and matplotlib for charts. The slides are LaTeX Beamer. Everything is in the repository and runs from the raw
files.

**I5. Can someone reproduce your results?** *(P3)*\
Yes. The raw files are in the repository exactly as downloaded, with checksums. One script builds the dataset,
another validates it, and the notebooks produce every table and chart. The notebooks are saved with their outputs,
so they can be read without running anything.

**I6. Which lecture was most useful?** *(P1)*\
Lecture 22 on offer curves, because it gave us the hypotheses. The benchmark comes from lecture 21's definition of
the terms of trade, and lectures 8, 17, 19 and 20 explain why India's exposure fell.

**I7. Where do the Leontief paradox or factor price equalisation fit in?** *(Any)*\
They don't, directly, and we did not force them in. Those lectures are about the pattern of trade and factor
returns; our question is about the price of trade. The parts of H-O we do use are endowments (India is oil-scarce)
and the time horizons from lecture 13.

**I8. Your hypotheses came from theory, but two failed. Is the theory wrong?** *(P1)*\
The theory was ambiguous, not wrong. For H3, offer curves say a world boom shifts both curves, so the sign was always
empirical. For H2, the asymmetry idea comes from studies of output, not of prices. What theory did predict
clearly, H1 and its size through the benchmark, the data confirm.
