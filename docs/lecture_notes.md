# Lecture notes

What each lecture covers, and what we take from it for the oil and terms-of-trade project. Relevance: **High** = appears on slides; **Medium** = viva notes; **Low** = background only.

---

### L05: Introduction to Trade Theories (Low)
- Covers trade patterns over time, trade fallacies (the "zero-sum" view), Smith, Ricardo, Melitz, evidence on openness and growth (Sachs–Warner; Frankel–Romer), and protectionism history.
- Cites the *Financial Express* finding that India has comparative advantage vs China in "food, agricultural raw materials, **fuels**, ores, metals".
- **For us:** that fuels point supports our RCA calculation for refined petroleum (L08).

### L06: Mercantilism (Low)
- Covers bullionism, trade surplus as the goal, trade as a zero-sum game, and the Physiocrats.
- Reading list names **"RBI, DGCIS and IMF reports"**, the same official sources we use for data.
- **For us (viva):**
  - An oil shock widens India's trade deficit. A mercantilist would call that the loss. Modern theory (L11) says the real loss is the **fall in purchasing power from worse ToT**.
  - Gold (bullion) is a large Indian import, so we control for the gold price.

### L07: Absolute Advantage (Low)
- Covers Smith's absolute advantage and the U.S.–India wheat/cloth example.
- **For us:** background only.

### L08: Comparative Advantage and RCA (High)
- Covers Ricardo, opportunity cost, PPFs, gains from trade, and the equilibrium relative price lying between the two autarky prices.
- Defines the **Balassa RCA index**: RCA_ij = (X_ij / X_wj) / (X_i / X_w). RCA > 1 means comparative advantage. Sources named are UNCTADstat and WITS.
- **For us:** India has almost no crude but a strong **RCA in refined petroleum**: 0.06 in 1999, 1.26 in 2000 and 4.37 in 2025 (UNCTADstat). This acquired advantage is what partly *hedges* India's ToT against oil shocks.

### L11: Neoclassical / Standard Trade Model (High)
- Covers the increasing-cost PPF, MRT, community indifference curves, autarky equilibrium, the trade line, and gains from trade.
- Covers the **small vs large country** distinction: a small country trades at given world prices.
- **For us:**
  - India's ToT is the slope of its trade line. A worse ToT rotates the trade line inward, putting India on a lower indifference curve. That is a real-income loss even if production does not change.
  - Is India a price-taker in oil? India is a top oil consumer, which raises an endogeneity point. Our shock identification uses OPEC announcement shocks, which are exogenous to India.

### L12: Heckscher–Ohlin Theory (Medium)
- Covers factor intensity, factor abundance, the H-O theorem, and factor price equalization.
- **For us:** India is poorly endowed with oil (a natural-resource "factor"), so crude imports are **structural**. ToT exposure cannot be traded away quickly, only managed.

### L13: HOS and Income Distribution (Medium)
- Covers Stolper–Samuelson, the **Ricardo–Viner** specific-factors model, Cairnes–Haberler, and **time horizons**: very short run (all factors fixed), medium run (specific factors), long run (HOS).
- **For us:**
  - An oil price rise raises returns to factors specific to refining and hurts oil-using sectors (distribution effects).
  - The short run / long run split maps onto our **error-correction model** (short-run vs long-run coefficients).

### L14: Empirical Testing of H-O (Medium)
- Covers the Leontief paradox, factor intensity reversal, and **transport costs** (which drive a wedge between countries' prices).
- **For us:** import unit values are CIF (cost, insurance, freight); export unit values are FOB (free on board). Freight and insurance spikes, such as war-risk premiums, also worsen measured ToT. This is a viva point and a known limitation.

### L15: Extensions of H-O (Low)
- Covers the product cycle, intra-firm trade, outsourcing, and transport costs (partial vs general equilibrium).
- **For us:** background only.

### L16: Imperfect Competition I (High)
- Covers internal and external economies of scale and the **monopoly markup**: MR = p(1 − 1/e) = MC, so the price is above marginal cost by a margin set by the demand elasticity. Also covers the pro-competitive gains from trade.
- **For us:**
  - OPEC+ behaves like a dominant supplier. World oil demand is price-inelastic in the short run, so the markup is large and supply cuts raise the price sharply.
  - The supply-shock series we use (Känzig 2021) is **built from OPEC announcements**.
  - When India diversifies to more suppliers (e.g., Russian crude since 2022), that works like a **pro-competitive effect** that lowers its effective import price.

### L17: Monopolistic Competition (Krugman model) (Medium)
- Covers the Krugman model, intra- vs inter-industry trade, and the warning that **"the more broadly an industry is defined, the more trade appears to be intra-industry"**. Also covers Melitz.
- **For us:** India both imports and exports "mineral fuels" (HS chapter 27). That looks like intra-industry trade at a broad level but is inter-industry (crude vs products) at a finer level. See L19–L20.

### L18: Intra-Industry Trade (Medium)
- Covers horizontal vs vertical IIT, India's IIT, and the immiserizing growth mention (Bhagwati 1958).
- **For us (viva):** if India's growth raises its oil import demand enough to worsen its ToT, part of the growth gain leaks abroad. That is the immiserizing-growth logic.

### L19: Measuring IIT I (High)
- Covers the **Grubel–Lloyd index**: GL = 1 − |X − M| / (X + M), between 0 and 1. Covers its limits (trade imbalance; it cannot separate horizontal from vertical IIT).
- **For us:** GL for India's oil trade is 0.55 when crude and products count as one industry and 0.10 when HS 2709 and 2710 are measured separately (UN Comtrade, 2025). That is the aggregation effect.

### L20: Measuring IIT II (High)
- Covers horizontal vs vertical IIT using a **unit-value dispersion rule** (Abd-el-Rahman ±15%; GHM; Fontagné–Freudenberg; Azhar–Elliott).
- **For us:** compare India's export unit value of refined products with its import unit value of crude. The gap shows **vertical differentiation / value addition**: India imports a low-value input and exports a higher-value product.

### L21: Partial Equilibrium Analysis (High)
- Covers partial vs general equilibrium, excess supply/demand, and the equilibrium price.
- Covers **aggregation bias** (the apples/oranges example).
- **Defines the net barter ToT:** "In a world of many traded goods, the terms of trade is the ratio of the export price index to the import price index, also called commodity or net barter terms of trade." This is exactly the DGCI&S/RBI series we use.
- **For us:**
  - The PE oil market explains the **oil import bill**: inelastic demand means price spikes raise the bill with little volume change.
  - The aggregation-bias point is why we separate oil from non-oil trade.

### L22: Offer Curves (High: central framework)
- Covers offer curves (reciprocal demand), the ToT equilibrium where the curves cross, and disequilibrium adjustment.
- Covers **shifts:**
  - Higher import demand by A: trade volume rises and A's ToT falls.
  - Higher import demand by B: B's ToT falls and A's ToT improves.
  - A tariff: trade volume falls and the imposing country's ToT improves.
  - Supply changes (productivity, resource discovery) shift the curves.
- **For us:** India vs rest of world (incl. OPEC+) offer curves:
  - An oil supply cut is like OPEC "restricting trade": its curve shifts in, so India's ToT falls.
  - A world demand boom raises demand for India's exports too, so the effect is smaller.
  - India's own demand growth shifts India's curve out, so India's ToT falls.
